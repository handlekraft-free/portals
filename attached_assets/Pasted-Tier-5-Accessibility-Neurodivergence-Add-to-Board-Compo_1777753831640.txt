Tier 5: Accessibility & Neurodivergence (Add to Board Composition Plan)
13. Web Accessibility & Inclusive Design Expertise
The technical and design competency to ensure handlekraft's deliverables meet accessibility standards. WCAG 2.2 compliance is the floor; genuine inclusive design is the goal. This person should be able to review your client deliverables and curriculum from an accessibility standpoint, advise on tooling decisions (screen reader compatibility, keyboard navigation, color contrast, semantic HTML), and prevent the common failure mode where accessibility is bolted on at the end rather than designed in from the start.
This matters legally — Title III of the ADA and California's Unruh Civil Rights Act create real liability for inaccessible websites, and that liability flows to whoever built them as well as whoever owns them, even with the contractual structure you discussed with Brian. It also matters strategically — clients in the disability-services sector won't engage you if they can't trust your work.
Look for: senior accessibility specialist, IAAP-certified Web Accessibility Specialist (WAS) or Certified Professional in Accessibility Core Competencies (CPACC), inclusive design researcher, or assistive technology developer. Bonus if they've worked at organizations like Microsoft's accessibility team, Adobe Inclusive Design, Knowbility, or the Bureau of Internet Accessibility.
14. Lived Experience: Disability and Neurodivergence
Distinct from professional expertise — this is a board member who is themselves disabled or neurodivergent, who can speak from authentic experience about what it's like to navigate work, education, and technology with their particular condition or community. This person grounds the board's understanding of your fellowship participants and the end users of your client organizations' work. Without this voice, board conversations about accessibility tend to drift toward technical compliance rather than genuine human service.
The disability community has a clear ethic on this: "Nothing about us without us." A board doing work that affects disabled or neurodivergent people without including disabled or neurodivergent voices on the board itself is replicating exactly the exclusion you're trying to address.
Look for: a community member, advocate, or professional who is openly disabled or neurodivergent and willing to bring that perspective to board service. Disability rights organizations, neurodivergent professional networks (e.g., autism advocacy groups, ADHD professional associations), and disability-led nonprofits are good places to start. Be aware that many qualified candidates may have hesitations about board service due to past tokenization experiences — be specific about why their voice matters and how it will be used.
15. Inclusive Workforce Development & Universal Design for Learning
The pedagogical and program-design competency for serving neurodivergent learners well. This is different from web accessibility (#13) and lived experience (#14) — it's expertise in how learning environments, instruction methods, assessment practices, and workplace accommodations need to be structured to genuinely include people whose minds work differently.
Universal Design for Learning (UDL) is the framework that comes closest to what handlekraft needs. It treats accommodation as a default design principle rather than as an exception process. For an organization training non-traditional learners — many of whom will be neurodivergent, have learning differences, or have disabilities that affect how they engage with traditional instruction — UDL competency on the board protects you from designing programs that work only for neurotypical learners.
This matters directly for handlekraft's fellowship: pacing, mentor structure, asynchronous components, sensory considerations during synchronous sessions, evaluation methods, and onboarding rhythm all benefit from informed design choices. It also matters for hiring and retention — many of your future fellowship graduates and team members will be neurodivergent.
Look for: special education or vocational rehabilitation professional, disability employment specialist, UDL practitioner from higher education or training contexts, or organizational consultant who specializes in neurodivergent workforce inclusion. Organizations like the Job Accommodation Network (JAN), state Departments of Rehabilitation, and disability:IN have networks of professionals working in this space.
